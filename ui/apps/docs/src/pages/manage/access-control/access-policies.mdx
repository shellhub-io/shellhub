# Access Policies

An identity proves who is connecting. A policy decides what they may reach.

Policies are namespace-scoped and evaluated on every connection. Evaluation is **default deny**:
until a policy grants access, nothing is reachable, and a namespace with no policies refuses
everything.

Manage them under **Access Policies** in the console.

<Shot id="access-policies" route="/access-policies" of={{ role: "table" }} alt="The Access Policies page listing policies with their effect, subject, and targets" />

## Anatomy

**Name** — how you will recognize it. It appears verbatim in the denial when a deny refuses
somebody, so a descriptive name is what makes a refusal diagnosable. `Operators to prod` beats
`policy-2`.

**Effect** — `allow` grants the access described; `deny` blocks it. A matching deny wins over any
allow, however specific.

**Subject** — who it applies to:

| Subject | Applies to |
|---|---|
| All members | Every member of the namespace |
| Role | Everyone holding that role, so the grant follows the role rather than the person |
| User | One person |
| Service account | An automated system connecting on its own identity |

<Callout variant="warning">
**All members excludes service accounts.** A broad `all-members` allow will not sweep
a service account in by accident. Grant one on purpose, with a user or role subject. A role
subject already excludes them by exact match.
</Callout>

**Devices** — which devices are covered:

| Selector | Behavior |
|---|---|
| All devices | Every device in the namespace, including ones enrolled later |
| Tags | Devices carrying the selected tags |

**Logins** — which unix logins are allowed on those devices: a specific list like `deploy` and
`root`, or any. This is the difference between reaching a device and reaching it as root.

**Source IP** — optionally, only accept connections from these addresses or ranges. Empty matches
any address. It only ever **narrows** — it never grants.

**Re-authentication** — optionally, require the identity to confirm in the browser before
connecting. Either every login, or once per window. See
[Login Approvals](/manage/access-control/login-approvals).

## How policies combine

Every deny is evaluated first. If one matches, the connection is refused and no allow overrides
it.

If no deny matches, the allows are evaluated, and one match is enough.

If nothing matches, refused.

A deny whose selector cannot be evaluated fails closed. An allow that cannot be evaluated is
treated as a non-match.

That order makes a working policy set predictable in shape: **broad allows for what people
normally need, then narrow denies to carve out exceptions.** Fighting a deny by adding more allows
does not work.

## A worked example

A team where operators handle the fleet, one contractor touches only lab devices, and a pipeline
deploys.

| Name | Effect | Subject | Devices | Logins |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Operators to fleet | allow | Role: operator | tag `production` | `deploy` |
| Contractor to lab | allow | User: contractor | tag `lab` | any |
| No root anywhere | deny | All members | All devices | `root` |
| Pipeline deploys | allow | Service account `ci-bot` | tag `production` | `deploy` |

What this buys you:

- The deny spans the namespace and needs no exception list. It beats all three allows without any
  of them being rewritten.
- Tagging a new device `production` grants operators and the pipeline at once. No policy is
  edited.
- Adding somebody to the operator role grants them the fleet. No policy is edited.
- `ci-bot` needs its own allow — the `all-members` deny still applies to it, but no `all-members`
  *allow* would have covered it.

## Starting from scratch

A new namespace has one policy: **Owner access**, granting the owner every login on every device.
Everybody else has nothing.

A reasonable second and third policy:

1. An allow for the role your team mostly holds, scoped to a tag, with the logins they actually
   need — not `*`.
2. A namespace-wide deny for `root`, if root is not something people should be reaching for
   routinely.

Then add narrower allows as real needs appear, rather than trying to model the whole organization
up front.
