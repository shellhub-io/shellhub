# Service Accounts

A service account is a principal with no human behind it — a non-human identity that only holds
SSH identities. Use one for anything that connects on its own: a pipeline, a monitoring job, a
deployment runner.

## Why not just use a person's key

Because of what happens later.

A pipeline configured with an engineer's key works fine until that engineer leaves. Then their
identity is revoked, and the pipeline breaks — at the worst possible moment, with a failure whose
cause is not obvious.

Before that, every deploy in the audit trail is attributed to a person who was not deploying, so
the record is actively misleading.

A service account fixes both. The audit trail says `ci-bot`. Revoking a person touches nothing the
automation depends on.

## Creating one

On the **SSH Identities** page, use **Add a Key** and choose to add it for a new service account
rather than for yourself. Name it for the system — `ci-bot`, `backup-runner` — and paste the
public key the automation will connect with.

## What a service account is not

It carries a role with **no permissions and no authority**. A service account cannot administer
the namespace, manage members, or do anything in the console.

It exists to hold SSH identities and connect. That is the whole scope, and it is deliberate: an
automated principal with administrative reach is a much larger problem when its key leaks.

## Granting access

Service accounts need their own policy, and this is the part that trips people up.

**An `all-members` allow does not cover a service account.** The carve-out is deliberate — it is
the one place a non-human principal could be swept into an over-broad grant by accident.

Grant one on purpose, with a subject naming the service account:

| Name | Effect | Subject | Devices | Logins |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pipeline deploys | allow | Service account `ci-bot` | tag `production` | `deploy` |

Note that `all-members` **deny** policies still apply. A namespace-wide "no root anywhere" deny
covers service accounts too, which is what you want.

## Key lifecycle

Two limits that exist mainly for service accounts:

**Expiry.** Give the key a deadline, so an automation nobody has touched in a year does not still
hold live credentials.

**Single use.** Only offered for a service account's key: one key serves one automated run, and
once consumed it is spent. For a genuinely one-shot job — a provisioning run, a migration — this
is the tightest thing available.

## No re-authentication

A policy's `require_reauth` never applies to a service account. Re-auth is an interactive browser
step and there is nobody to complete it; demanding one would simply hang the connection.

Freshness for a service account comes from its key's lifecycle — expiry and single use — not from
a periodic prompt. If a policy path needs re-auth, it needs a human on it.
