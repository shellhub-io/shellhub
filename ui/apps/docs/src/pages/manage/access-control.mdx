# How Authorization Works

Every connection is authorized before the device is contacted. ShellHub refuses at the server,
before it mints anything for the agent, so a denied login never reaches the device at all.

How the decision is made depends on which model your namespace uses.

## Two models

| | Identity | Legacy |
|---|---|---|
| What proves who you are | An [SSH identity](/manage/access-control/ssh-identities) bound to a person or service account | A public key registered in the namespace |
| What decides what you reach | [Access policies](/manage/access-control/access-policies) | The key's device ACL, plus firewall rules |
| An unlisted device is | Denied | Allowed, unless a rule blocks it |
| The audit trail names | The principal | The key |

**Namespaces created today start in identity mode and stay there** — a namespace born in identity
mode cannot be switched back. Namespaces that predate identity mode default to legacy and can move
across when you are ready.

The rest of this page describes identity mode. For the older model see
[Public Keys](/manage/access-control/public-keys) and
[Firewall Rules](/manage/access-control/firewall-rules); to move, see
[Migrating from the Legacy Model](/manage/access-control/migrating-from-legacy).

## Identities prove who

An SSH identity binds a public key to a **principal** — a person on your team, or a service
account. The key is the credential; the identity is who that credential belongs to.

This inversion is what makes everything downstream work. In the legacy model a key *is* the
access: whoever holds it gets in, and the record can only name the key. With identities, a
connection whose key fingerprint resolves to an identity is recognized as that principal, and
policy evaluation, the session record and revocation are all about the person.

See [SSH Identities](/manage/access-control/ssh-identities).

## Policies decide what

An identity proves who. A policy decides what they may reach: a subject, a set of devices, a set
of unix logins, and whether the effect is to allow or deny.

See [Access Policies](/manage/access-control/access-policies) for writing them, and
[Policy Reference](/manage/access-control/policy-reference) for every field.

## How a decision is made

When a connection arrives, ShellHub resolves the presented key to an identity, then evaluates the
namespace's policies against that identity, the target device, and the requested unix login.

The order:

1. **Membership.** Not a member of the namespace → refused, before policies are consulted.
2. **Every deny.** A matching deny refuses, and nothing later overrides it. A deny that cannot be
   evaluated also refuses.
3. **Every allow.** One match is enough. The strictest re-authentication requirement across all
   matching allows is taken.
4. **Nothing matched** → refused.

Three properties fall out of that, and they are the ones worth remembering:

**Default deny.** A device is unreachable until some policy says otherwise. A namespace with no
policies at all refuses every connection — and ShellHub short-circuits that case, refusing
immediately rather than proceeding toward a login that could never be authorized.

**Deny wins.** Deny is a subtractive blocklist carved out of broad grants, not a base layer.
Default deny already blocks everything a policy does not grant, so deny exists to carve exceptions
*out of* your allows.

**Deny fails closed.** If a deny's filter or source IP cannot be evaluated, ShellHub assumes it
applies. An allow that cannot be evaluated is treated as a non-match instead — the asymmetry is
deliberate, and it always errs toward refusing.

## Where the network does not fit

Network position is not part of the decision. There is no "inside": a device on your office LAN
and one on a customer's cellular link are evaluated identically, against the same policies.

A policy can carry a source IP restriction, but it only ever **narrows** a grant that already
matched. An empty list matches any address, and no source IP ever grants access on its own.

In identity mode the legacy firewall rules — which match on source IP, hostname and username —
are bypassed entirely. They remain stored and manageable, and are never consulted.

## The starter policy

A namespace in identity mode is seeded with one policy, **Owner access**, granting the namespace
owner every login on every device. It is created when a namespace is born in identity mode, and
also when a namespace switches to identity mode with no policies of its own.

Without it, default deny would lock the owner out of their own namespace at the moment the mode
changed. Every other member starts with no access at all.

## What authorization does not do

**It runs once, at the handshake.** Revoking an identity or deleting a policy does not tear down a
session that is already open — that session was authorized when it started, and it continues
until it is closed.

**The reason is not surfaced.** A refusal carries a specific reason internally, but the connecting
client receives a generic access-denied. [Connection Denied](/manage/access-control/connection-denied) works
through the four possibilities.
